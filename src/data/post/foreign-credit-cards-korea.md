Korea is one of the easiest countries in Asia to pay by card. Most shops, cafés, convenience stores and taxis take cards. But a few everyday things still need cash, and visitors often find out at the worst moment, like at a subway machine or a market stall.

This guide covers where your foreign card works, where it doesn't, and a few habits that save you money.

## Quick answer

| Situation                                | Foreign card?                        | What to do                                  |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Shops, cafés, restaurants in cities      | **Yes**, Visa and Mastercard work    | Carry one Visa or Mastercard as your main   |
| Big hotels, department stores            | Yes, Amex often works too            | Bring a backup card in case one is declined |
| Traditional markets, small food stalls   | **Often cash only**                  | Keep some Korean won (KRW) with you         |
| T-money top-up at a convenience store    | **No**, cash only                    | Top up with cash                            |
| T-money top-up at regular subway machine | **No**, cash only                    | Top up with cash                            |
| Korean shopping and delivery apps        | Sometimes, depends on the app        | Check the payment page before ordering      |
| ATMs                                     | Yes, at ATMs that take foreign cards | Look for a "Global ATM" sign                |

## Which card networks work

**Visa and Mastercard** are the safest choices. They are accepted almost everywhere that takes cards in Korea.

**American Express** works at major hotels and department stores, but smaller places may not take it. If Amex is your main card, bring a Visa or Mastercard as well.

Even with the right network, a foreign card is sometimes declined at smaller stores. This is usually the shop's terminal, not your card. Having a second card from a different bank helps.

## When you still need cash

Plan to carry some cash for:

- **Traditional markets** such as street food stalls and small vendors
- **Small family restaurants**, especially outside big cities
- **T-money top-ups** (see below)

You don't need much. A small amount of won for snacks, markets and top-ups is enough for most travelers, since cards cover the rest.

## T-money: the most common surprise

T-money is the rechargeable card most people use for subways and buses in Korea. You tap it when you get on and off.

The catch is that a **regular T-money card can't be topped up with a credit card**, Korean or foreign. At convenience stores and at standard subway top-up machines, you pay in cash.

Here is what you can do:

1. Buy a T-money card at a convenience store or the airport.
2. Top it up with cash, either at the counter ("T-money charge, please") or at a subway machine.
3. Newer Seoul subway machines accept foreign Visa and Mastercard for **single-journey tickets**, but not for T-money top-ups.

Some prepaid travel cards for foreigners, like WOWPASS, let you load money with a foreign card and also have a T-money function. Check the card's own page for current fees and where you can load it.

## Using ATMs with a foreign card

Not every Korean ATM accepts foreign cards. Look for machines marked **"Global ATM"**, or ones showing the Cirrus, Plus, Visa or Mastercard logos.

ATMs in train stations, bus terminals and department stores tend to be the most foreigner-friendly.

Two tips:

- Korean ATMs often expect a **4-digit PIN**. If your PIN is longer, ask your bank before you travel.
- Your home bank may charge its own fee on top of the ATM fee. Taking out larger amounts less often keeps fees down.

## Always pay in won, not your home currency

Sometimes a card machine or ATM asks if you want to pay in your home currency (for example USD) instead of Korean won. This is called **dynamic currency conversion**.

It looks convenient, but the exchange rate is usually worse than your card network's rate. **Choose KRW (won)** when asked.

## Korean apps and websites

Many Korean apps for delivery, shopping or tickets are built for Korean residents. Some ask for **본인인증** (identity verification with a Korean phone number) or only take Korean cards.

Before you spend time filling a cart, scroll to the payment page and check whether foreign cards, PayPal or other options are listed. If not, look for an English or international version of the service.

## Summary

- Bring **Visa or Mastercard** as your main card, plus one backup card.
- Keep **some cash** for markets, small restaurants and T-money top-ups.
- Use **Global ATMs** for cash and choose **KRW** when asked about currency.
- Expect some Korean apps to need a Korean phone number or card.
